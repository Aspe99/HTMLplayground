    Pianists

    I can be rather selective about who is performing. If the piece is slightly too fast, I turn it off and listen to sommething else. I am very picky. For example, I like Lang Lang playing 'La Campanella', as he is a very technically advanced pianist. There are some other recordings out there that I do like, as they emphasise certain parts more. Lang Lang plays the Coda very well, whereas other performers seem to like being quiet and playing stacato. On the other hand, I really don't like Lang Lang playing Fantasie Impromputu, as he shows off and plays it too quickly. Someone like 'German Kitkin' plays it much better. IMSLP is one of the best places to go to see different performers and listen to vastly different recordings. I find that the most popular person in terms of my saved recording on IMSLP is 'Luis Kolodin', which is apparently a stage name for 'Fellipe Sarro'.

    Variations On A Theme

    There is something in music called, 'Variations On A Theme'. This is where you take a theme of anything, and you change it slightly, working through, having your own changes to it. It is very interesting, and below I talk about some examples.

    • Starting off with a familiar one, we have 'La Campanella'. This originates from a violin piece by a show off called 'Paganini'. He wrote this. This is still called 'La Campanella'. This is the original theme. Liszt then came along and took out the parts that he didn't like, added what he did (and changed the key), wrote it for piano, and gave us this.
    • Next, we have Rachmaninoff. He also took a theme from Paganini (a different one I believe) and wrote Rhapsody on a Theme of Paganini. Here is Rubenstein playing the 18th Variation. This Variation is often described as Rachmaninoff's 'most beautiful' variation, and this video explains how he wrote it. This also provides a small explanation on creating variations of a theme.
    • Finally, this one is very nice, and is by British Composer, Edward Elgar wrote variations on an orignal theme. This means that he wrote the original theme, and then varied it. This word is more famously known as 'The Enigma Variations'. He wrote the original 'Enigma' in 1898. This is the 9th Variation (Variation IX) called 'Nimrod'. The entire set consists of 14 variations. Each one is a musical portrait of a specific friend or family member from Elgar's circle, identified in the score by their initials or a nickname. The name for the 'Nimrod Variation' is a playful reference to Elgar's close friend, his editor and publisher Augustus J. Jaeger. In the Bible, Nimrod was a "mighty hunter," and "Jaeger" is the German word for "hunter." Elgar wrote this variation to capture a deep, late-night conversation he and Jaeger had about the grandeur of Beethoven's slow movements. This is the full Enigma Variations.
